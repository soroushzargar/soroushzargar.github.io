There is no need to highlight the importance, and strength of AI in our daily tasks. AI has outperformed human in Chess, Go, some athletic skills, Coding, Optimization, and also recently it has published a paper at workshop in a top tier venue.

The question of "Is AI intelligent, or will it become intelligent" depends on a much harder, and often overlooked question of "what is intelligence?". But the difficulty of this question also tempts us to try and circumvent it. Can we discuss computer's intelligence without thinking of what intelligence is? This opens the door to several important ideas including "behaviorism" and "functionalism".

## Turing's Test

Behaviorism says that if a system acts like an intelligent person, then there is no reason to deny its intelligence. That is why Alan Turing calls a program intelligent if it passes the following test: Put the program on one side of a line, where the other side is an examiner. The examiner is told that on the other side of the line there is either a human or a computer program, and through chatting, they should decide which one it is. If the examiner cannot distinguish the program from an actual human being, then the program is intelligent. That is also called the imitation game, as we expect the system to imitate us. If that is the point of calling a program intelligent, then we are already there, as older models have passed this test [1].

The first critiques of this test ask: (1) Is intelligence just being like humans? What if the computer can accomplish cognitively demanding tasks even better than humans, but is just not able to talk like humans? If we have two programs, one much more sophisticated and competent than the other but worse at imitating, then should we call the better program less intelligent? (2) What is the human going to ask? Is it just a daily chat, or are they going to ask complicated and hard questions? What human should be called a correct judge in this case? (3) What if the system tends not to talk like normal human beings? In the study from Jones et al. [1], the same model fails the Turing test if it is not prompted to imitate a human speaking style. So the same program can simultaneously fail and succeed at the same test, just by changing a context that does not have anything to do with intelligence.

This underspecification, however, is completely natural. By the time Alan Turing lived, computer science was not so mature, technology was mostly analog, and computers were basically calculators. Namely, Turing himself invented the abstract model of computation, which is equivalent to anything that computes, including today's computers. On the foundation of his universal Turing machine, computer science answered the question of what problems are efficiently solvable, what is harder to solve, and what is impossible to compute at all. Therefore, taking this test as a measure to use or to criticize for asserting intelligence, is analogous to asking the inventor of the internal combustion engine about how to solve the overheating issue in the W16 engine of a Bugatti.

## Functionalism and symbols

Moving a bit deeper than "if a system acts like humans, it is intelligent," we find functionalism. It says that mental states can be similarly reproduced on other physical systems, which is formally called multiple realizability. Our mind is on a platform of neurons and chemical reactions; you can build another mind on circuits, as long as you can reproduce its states. You call your reproduction of a mental state (like pain or joy) successful, if it has the same cause and function. For instance, when we feel pain, it is because there is some malfunction happening: something is breaking our skin, or there is some organ not working properly. This state is designed to force us to solve the problem. If you can create a similar cause and effect, no matter how it is produced, it is "pain".

But this can even be more radical and lead to full characterization of intelligence. Let's look at how do we prove theorems in math: Basically we manipulate formulae and equations, under a grounded rule, and we end up at some point which is tautology. Different people can prove the same theorem differently and as long as they reach to the destination without breaking the rules, they are equally good. Same argument can apply to intelligent as well. Intelligent can be manipulation of symbols. If you stay within the grounded rules (do not conclude something stupidly) you are intelligent. Or are you?

## Is it enough to be competent?

Searle proposes a hypothetical experiment. Let's say you don't know any word from the Chinese language, and you are given a book that comprehensively tells you how to reply when you are asked a certain phrase, which to you is just a sequence of symbols. The bottom line is that if you purely rely on this book, you do not know the language even if you sound perfect in Chinese. It is like there is some inherent understanding that a rule-based setup does not carry.

## When the Chinese Book Argument Breaks

A critique from my POV to this example is the concept of latent variables. Under the hood of current machine learning models, there are internal representations, not understandable to us, that carry some sort of meaning. For example, each token in an LLM is assigned an embedding, an array of numbers, which can show some meaningful structure. In other words, semantic relations can be shown through adding and subtracting these embedding arrays. One interesting example is that in many models, if you do "King − Male + Female" over their embeddings, which are internal and learned during the training phase, you end up somewhere close to the embedding of "Queen." Such structure is never forced during training, yet it emerges. This signals that we can reject the assumption of a perfect rule-based system without any inherent understanding.

A more precise version of the Chinese Room argument is called the symbol grounding problem. Even if we assume that intelligence is defined as the manipulation of symbols in a more efficient way, and given that today's AI is competent in processing those symbols very effectively, still, from Harnad's perspective [4], the important question is the knowledge associated with the symbols. Is this knowledge genuine to the understanding of the model, or is it borrowed from our understanding? In many cases the data over which the model is trained is purely generated by humans. For instance current LLMs are trained on a large set of data generated by human being, like websites, books, reviews, academic papers, etc. An "apple" for an LLM is a mixture of understandings gained from people's writings about apples. If all people in the world decided to always call apples blue in their writings, even though they are not, the language model would have no way to understand that they are not blue. In more precise wording, the latent understanding of a symbol in the machine's mind is only borrowed from us. Note that here, by the word "us", I refer to the collective knowledge and representations for that symbol: all textbooks, blogs, course recordings, and other materials that human beings have so far produced. There is no such understanding of an apple if an apple is never touched or seen by the machine through any sensory mechanism that it has. This reduces intelligence to embodiment.

Why is embodiment an important factor? Dreyfus [5] argued that human expertise rests on a background of embodied, pre-reflective coping that cannot be captured by explicit rules and symbols. What AI is trained for in many cases is to find the symbol, statement, or judgment that has the highest probability w.r.t. what it has already read. It captures high correlations; e.g., it can say that clouds and rain are highly likely to appear at the same time, but the claim "clouds cause rain" is much harder to understand. Understanding can be achieved in some ways by finding counterfactual examples; for example, keeping everything the same, removing the clouds, and asking if it would still rain. The logic of causation has a component of intervention - changing attributes and observing what happens. Such an assumption is rarely achievable through training only on data. It requires the model to have agency over the event, so it is able to intervene.

## What does the AI Science tell Us?

From the perspective of machine learning, several observations are worth highlighting:

### Meaningfulness in the latent space

Every 1920×1080 set of pixels is displayable in full HD, but among the space of all these objects, only a tiny fraction are images; the rest is garbage. Interestingly, a machine learning model can find a lower-dimensional manifold over which the meaningful images are placed [6]. This meaningful manifold somehow suggests that the Chinese Room argument does not fully apply; however, in many cases, the inherent understanding is also a summarization of what we gave to the model. The model has not interacted or coped with the real world to understand what an apple is, but it has studied millions of images that hold a nice and rich enough understanding of such a concept. This suggests that, in these cases, the symbol grounding problem is still present.

### Adversarial Robustness

Another caveat is adversarial attacks [7]. These attacks are perturbations over an original inputs that, from a human perspective are imperceptible or at least do not change the semantics. Specially in case of images the perturbed and original image are identical to human perception, while the model entirely changes its prediction between them. These noises are crafted in a way that break the arithmetic that caused the correct behavior and shift it toward another direction. As you can see in the figure, the noise added to the panda is perfectly crafted given the weights of the model, in order to shift its prediction from panda to gibbon. The noise is not visible to our eye, as the images are identical for us. The bottom line is that such an attack is imperceptible to the human eye, which suggests that the model does not pay enough attention to semantics, as it can be easily fooled.

But how do adversarial attacks confuse the model? They use the limitations of the structure and optimize for the worst prediction, and in our current networks, the worst prediction is somewhere close to the correct one. The question is: are there such examples that fool the human brain? Something that leverages the limitations of our neural system and makes us infer something that is definitely not true? Yes, a good example is optical illusions. It could potentially be that these examples are rare as we do not have a white box access to human brain, or the architecture is so complicated and perfectly designed that make these examples rare.

### Agency as a key to training

While the common setup for training a model is supervised, meaning that the model is given a large set of inputs and desired outputs to mimic the reference judgment, there is also a learning regime called reinforcement learning. One highlighted example are models outperforming world champions in chess and Go. In reinforcement learning, there is no predefined set of inputs or outputs. The model behaves through some policy (some set of internal judgment), which perhaps in the beginning is a terrible one, and in retrospect, it receives some reward from the environment. For example, in chess, the model may decide to move a pawn four steps forward. Since this is a false move, the environment punishes the model with a reward of -10 and moves back the falsely moved piece. Over time, the model trains itself to maximize the reward it receives from the environment. This is a form of learning in which the model has agency: it interacts with the environment, and upon interaction, it curates signals to find a better policy and update itself.

Now, if in a game of chess you block the players' view of their opponent's face, the only interface they interact with is the board. The same interface is also available to the AI model, and it can play with it. So it seems like, in this particular task, the model has the same embodiment as a human. Another example is coding. Removing the feeling of the keyboard, the colors on the screen, and all the other presentations, what we interact with is a sequence of words, and our reward is whether our code works correctly and efficiently. The playground does not have any difference from what coding AI agents have access to.

### Adaptability, and discovery

Complicated words, but a simple concept. Our brain is adaptively correcting, or better to say changing, itself. It interacts with the world environment, gets a reward from events, such as hurting itself, benefiting from something, fear, loss, joy, etc., and rewires itself to gain more of the good feelings. In many of today's AI models, the core is the same and what changes is the context. You use the same model for your coding assistant as I am using to polish this piece. My ChatGPT is the same as yours. This means that, at least for commercial models, they do not adapt.

However, models can adapt when there is some verifiable signal. An example is policy optimization through reinforcement learning. Let's say the objective is to write code that accomplishes a task in the quickest way possible. Here, the signal is achieved by running and profiling the code, which is the same reference signal for us and for AI. In these cases, one approach is to roll out 10 solutions and train the model to produce the top 5 with higher probability and the worst 5 with lower probability. Now the model trained on this, has adapted itself. This adaptation is based on interaction with the environment, effectively so that it the new model state produces better answers with higher chance. Interestingly, in this task also, the model can outperform human beings.

## Conclusion

From my point of view, answering the question of whether AI is intelligent or will become intelligent at some point is a question of architecture. What components does the AI have access to? Is it a solid set of data, or is it an environment where AI can play with things, and receive verifiable signals? I guess discussing intelligence in the context of general or narrow AI is just deferring the same conclusion. Intelligence can also be defined as task-specific regimes, and this umbrella can expand as needed to cover basically everything. AI shows the ability to adapt, self-improve, and optimize when a good enough signal is at hand. So, by my judgment, AI is already becoming intelligent. However this adaptability to this day comes with a huge cost!

As a future question, let's ask what does it mean for us? I think the answer to this question is beyond the scope of this discussion, but I believe in a co-evolution cycle. We get stronger, more complicated things become automated, and this cycle grows over time. Consider the fact that a while ago, being able to calculate the root of numbers quickly was an intellectual ability, but do you remember how you did it? Exactly! Tasks lose their priority and new tasks emerge, as of today, you have much more complicated and important tasks to do rather than doing what a basic Casio calculator does.
